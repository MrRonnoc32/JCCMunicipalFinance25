Chapter 1 · Money in

Jacksonville raises less money than its peers

Jacksonville raised $2,069 per resident from its own sources in 2025. The middle peer raised $3,269. No peer raised less.

A city government gets money in a few ways. It taxes property and sales. It charges fees for services such as trash pickup and permits. It also gets grants from the state and federal governments. "Own-source revenue" means everything the city raises itself, without the grants.

Jacksonville trails on almost every one of these. Local taxes came to $1,557 per resident, against a peer median of $2,606. Property tax was $1,060 against $1,776. Charges for services were $239 against $740.

Part of the property tax gap is the rate. The city levied 11.32 mills in 2025 (cut to 11.19 for 2026), out of a possible 20. Part is the tax base, since Florida's homestead exemptions and caps hold taxable value below market value. One source few peers match: JEA paid $137.4 million into the General Fund, about $127 per resident.

Revenue per resident is not your tax bill

These numbers divide what each government collects by its population. They show how much a government has to work with. Businesses, visitors, and owners who live elsewhere pay part of every city's taxes. Orange County's tourist tax alone brought in about $250 per county resident, against about $29 in Duval.

Florida law closes many doors

Of ten revenue tools the peers use, five are closed to Jacksonville by state law (such as meals, vehicle, and income taxes). Three more are at or near their caps. What remains is the property tax, a sales surtax by referendum, and fees.

Chapter 2 · Money out

Public safety comes first, and little is left for the rest

Jacksonville spent $2,065 per resident in 2025. The core peer median was $3,755. No peer, core or extended, spent less.

55%

$982
Public safety spending per resident. Core peer median: $1,627.
$56
Culture and recreation per resident. Core peer median: $236. This is the widest gap in the study.

Police, fire, and corrections take 47.6% of Jacksonville's spending. That is 8.7 points above the typical core peer. But in dollars, Jacksonville spends less on public safety than most peers do. The budget is not lavish on public safety. It is small everywhere else.

Spending is not the same as service. Cities differ in what they provide, what they pay workers, and how well they run. This study measures dollars, not results. Still, a gap this large is hard to close through efficiency alone.

A caution on 2025 service figures

The city's 2025 statements moved about $300 million between service areas compared with 2024. The most likely cause is a change in how pension and central costs were assigned. Three findings hold either way: public safety's share is above the median, culture and recreation is below it, and total spending is the lowest among core peers. The transportation gap does not hold on the 2024 basis.

Side note

How we picked the peers

Most city comparisons start with a list someone chose. This one started with data. The Research Office grouped U.S. metro areas by how alike they are across many traits at once, then kept the ones that landed with Jacksonville every time.

  1. 1. Start wideHundreds of U.S. metro areas, each described by population, growth, income, density, age, education, and industry mix.
  2. 2. Group by likenessA clustering method (k-means) sorted metros into groups of look-alikes. It was run many ways to test how stable each match was.
  3. 3. Check it twiceThe whole test was repeated with a second, unrelated source: Census survey data, then OECD data. Only metros that matched Jacksonville in both were kept.
  4. 4. Core and extendedThe 7 most stable matches are the core peers. Six more are extended peers. Headline findings use the core set.

Core peers (7): San Antonio, Las Vegas, Virginia Beach, Tampa, Orlando, Sacramento, Richmond.

Extended peers (6): Charlotte, Nashville, Tucson, Memphis, Raleigh, Austin.

Making them comparable: Jacksonville is a merged city and county. So each peer city is paired with its county, using the county's spending times the city's share of county residents. School districts and city-owned utilities like JEA are left out everywhere.

Chapter 2, continued · Pensions and debt

Pension debt is the weight Jacksonville carries

Jacksonville owes $4.04 billion more in promised pensions than it has saved. That is $3,747 per resident, the most of any government in the study.

47.2%
How funded the Police and Fire pension plan is. A fully funded plan is at 100%.
106.9%
The city's 2025 Police and Fire pension payment as a share of those workers' pay.
−$627
Net position per resident. Jacksonville is the only government studied that owes more than it owns.

A net pension liability is the gap between what a government has promised retirees and what it has set aside. Jacksonville's gap equals 181% of the money it raises itself in a year. For the typical core peer, it is closer to 37%.

In 2025, for every dollar paid in salary to an officer or firefighter, the city paid more than another dollar into their pension fund. The 2017 reform closed the old plans to new hires and set aside a future half-cent sales tax to pay down the debt.

Bonded debt is a different story. Jacksonville's $2,025 per resident is close to the $2,059 median. Yearly debt payments of $177 per resident are below the $257 median. That suggests room to borrow, but the report has not tested how much debt the city can afford.

Why this matters for everything else

Money that goes to pension payments cannot pay for roads, parks, or libraries. The report treats the pension, not bonded debt, as the main limit on Jacksonville's budget.

Chapter 3 · Top-ranked cities

Against top-ranked U.S. cities, Jacksonville is last of 15

The middle city in this group spends $4,226 per resident. Jacksonville spends $2,065.

The peers in Chapter 2 are cities like Jacksonville today. This chapter asks a different question: how does Jacksonville compare with the cities it hopes to join? We used the 2025 Oxford Economics Global Cities Index and took the 14 U.S. cities ranked at or near its top 50. We left out the very largest metros (New York, Los Angeles, Chicago) because of their size, and Washington, D.C., because it is a federal district.

Richer cities can spend more, so we also measured spending against residents' income. Jacksonville spends about $33.65 of every $1,000 its residents earn. Minneapolis, Portland, and Salt Lake City spend about $79. A few high-ranked cities (Austin, San Jose, Madison) spend at or near Jacksonville's level.

This shows spending, not cause

More spending will not by itself raise Jacksonville's rank. A city's standing depends a lot on its economy and workforce. The cause could also run the other way: strong economies let cities raise and spend more. What the chart shows is how far Jacksonville's public spending sits below the cities it wants to join.

Sources and limits

Source: JCC Research Office, How Jacksonville Pays for Itself: A Peer-City Comparison of Municipal Finances, Fiscal Year 2025, version 4 (October 2026). Figures come from each government's fiscal year 2025 Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR), the U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis Regional Price Parities for 2024, and the Oxford Economics Global Cities Index 2025.

  • All dollar figures are governmental activities per resident. K-12 schools and city-owned utilities are excluded.
  • Peer cities are paired with their counties. County amounts are scaled by the city's share of county population.
  • The price adjustment uses a consumer price index. It is only a rough guide to what governments pay for salaries and construction. Jacksonville's prices sit near the national average (99.5, where 100 is average).
  • The study measures spending and revenue, not service quality or outcomes. It does not estimate who finally pays each tax, whether fees cover costs, or how much debt the city can afford.
  • Some peers rely on separate districts (for example, Las Vegas's library district) that do not appear in city or county reports. If anything, peer spending is understated.

The Jacksonville Civic Council produces this research to give civic and business leaders a factual base for policy conversations. For questions, contact the JCC Research Office.
